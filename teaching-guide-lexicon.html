Model Fundamentals

How a language model sees, represents, and processes text.

01 Tokenization Fundamentals ▼
An AI model breaks input text into smaller units called tokens — which may be words, syllables, or characters — before processing.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Every word you type to an AI gets converted into numerical tokens. ‘Myocardial infarction’ might become 3–4 tokens. This matters clinically because AI models have token limits — long clinical notes may get truncated. Unusual medical terms can tokenize poorly, affecting comprehension.
For Researchers & Educators
Modern LLMs use byte-pair encoding (BPE) or SentencePiece. Rare terms fragment into subword tokens, creating longer sequences. Since attention complexity scales O(n²) with sequence length, tokenization efficiency directly affects compute cost and effective context.
Clinical Application
Roughly 1 token ≈ 0.75 English words. A 100-page protocol may exceed a model’s context limit, forcing chunking strategies that degrade extraction quality.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: a pharmacist reading a prescription — breaking it into recognizable units (drug, dose, frequency) before acting on it. Parsing quality determines response quality.
Related Concepts
EmbeddingsLatent SpaceRAG
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Tokenization is the entry point for understanding how AI reads. Educators who skip this concept leave learners confused when AI truncates notes, mishandles rare terminology, or produces unexpected output. It grounds all discussion of context windows, cost, and performance degradation on complex clinical text.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“The AI reads words like I do.” — It reads fragmented numeric tokens. Rare clinical terms fragment poorly. Longer prompts can silently exceed token limits and truncate critical information without any warning.

02 Embeddings Fundamentals ▼
Numerical representations of words, sentences, or concepts as vectors in high-dimensional space, where semantic similarity corresponds to geometric proximity.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Embeddings are how AI ‘understands’ meaning. ‘MI’ and ‘myocardial infarction’ have similar embeddings because they mean the same thing — learned from training data. This powers semantic search: a query for ‘chest pain’ can surface relevant ‘ACS’ records.
For Researchers & Educators
Tokens map to continuous vectors (typically 768–4096 dimensions). Semantic relationships emerge as geometric regularities. In biomedical NLP, domain-specific embeddings (BioWordVec, PubMedBERT) substantially outperform general embeddings on clinical tasks.
Clinical Application
Embeddings power semantic similarity in research corpora. In systematic-review automation, embedding-based screening surfaces studies that keyword search misses. Always choose an embedding model aligned with your clinical domain.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: a map where related medical concepts sit near each other — ‘hypertension’ close to ‘stroke,’ far from ‘fracture.’ Distance on the map = semantic distance.
Related Concepts
TokenizationLatent SpaceRAGAttention Mechanism
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Embeddings explain why AI can semantically search medical records and why synonym handling usually works — but also why subtle distinctions between similar conditions can fail. It is the conceptual bridge between raw text and AI ‘understanding,’ directly explaining how clinical AI search tools work.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“AI understands medical concepts like a doctor does.” — Embeddings encode statistical co-occurrence, not biological knowledge. ‘MI’ and ‘myocardial infarction’ are similar because they appear in similar textual contexts — not because the model understands cardiac pathophysiology.

03 Attention Mechanism Fundamentals ▼
The core operation in transformer models that lets the AI weigh the relevance of different parts of its input when generating each token of output.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Attention is why AI holds context across a long note. In ‘the patient takes metoprolol for his atrial fibrillation,’ ‘his’ correctly refers to ‘patient’ via attention. Longer notes mean more attention compute — and sometimes degraded performance at extremes.
For Researchers & Educators
Self-attention computes Q, K, V from input embeddings, scoring softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V. Multi-head attention runs parallel heads, letting the model attend to multiple relationship types simultaneously. This is what makes transformers powerful for long clinical documents.
Clinical Application
Attention patterns in clinical NLP can sometimes be interrogated for interpretability — identifying which note sections most influenced an output. Useful for explainability requirements, though attention does not equal causality.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: the selective focus of an experienced clinician reading a note — they zoom in on relevant history, key values, and priors depending on the specific decision at hand.
Related Concepts
EmbeddingsLatent SpaceResidual StreamsTokenization
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Attention explains both what transformers do well (long-range context, coreference resolution) and where they degrade (very long documents, adversarial inputs). Teaching this prevents learners from treating AI as a black box and gives them a framework for predicting where clinical note-reading will succeed or fail.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“Attention means the AI understands context like a human.” — Attention is a mathematical weighting operation learned from statistical co-occurrence. The model learns which tokens to weight together from training data patterns, not from semantic comprehension.

04 Latent Space Fundamentals ▼
The high-dimensional internal space inside an AI model where concepts, relationships, and meanings are encoded as geometric patterns.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Every interaction translates your words into a location in a vast abstract space where similar concepts cluster. The model ‘thinks’ in this space before converting back to words. Inputs from under-represented populations may fall in sparse, unreliable regions — a core source of AI health disparities.
For Researchers & Educators
Latent space is the learned representation manifold in activation space. Probing classifiers and activation patching suggest that high-level concepts (sentiment, factuality, named entities) are linearly separable — the premise of mechanistic interpretability research.
Clinical Application
Notes from under-represented demographics may fall in sparse latent-space regions, yielding degraded performance without any visible error signal. This is why demographic subgroup validation is non-negotiable before deploying any clinical AI.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: an MRI of the AI’s knowledge — you can’t see the thinking directly, but you can study patterns in the signal to understand where performance failures are structurally likely to occur.
Related Concepts
EmbeddingsAttention MechanismScaling LawsBias (AI)
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Latent space provides the mechanistic foundation for health equity in AI. Understanding why AI fails on under-represented populations — sparse representation — is more powerful than telling learners ‘AI can be biased.’ It gives them vocabulary to evaluate validation studies and challenge deployment decisions.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“AI is equally reliable for all patients.” — Performance drops in sparse latent-space regions without warning — no error flag, just degraded reliability. This is structurally baked into the training distribution, not fixable by prompting.

05 Residual Streams Fundamentals ▼
The running information highway inside a transformer — a vector that gets read from and written to by successive layers as the model processes input.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Residual streams are the model’s working memory during computation — each layer reads the current state, adds its contribution, and passes it forward. Disruptions (from model updates, quantization, or adversarial input) can cause unpredictable output changes even when the input looks identical.
For Researchers & Educators
Each transformer layer performs a read-modify-write on a shared residual stream (Elhage et al., 2021). This framing enables mechanistic interpretability: attention heads and MLPs compose circuits that implement discrete computational algorithms in-stream.
Clinical Application
For research users, this concept explains why model versioning matters: a vendor update changes what each layer writes to the stream, potentially changing outputs on identical inputs. Pinning a specific model version in research protocols is methodological necessity.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: a clinical handoff document successively annotated by each team member — each layer adds without erasing, and the final document reflects all contributions. A gap in any handoff degrades the final clinical picture.
Related Concepts
Attention MechanismLatent SpaceEmbeddings
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
For clinical educators, residual streams are most relevant for one practical insight: model updates can unpredictably change behavior on identical inputs. This directly motivates version pinning in research protocols.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“If I use the same AI tool, I’ll get the same results.” — An updated model version writes different information to the residual stream at each layer. Outputs on identical inputs can change substantially after a vendor update, with no visible notification to the user.

06 Scaling Laws Fundamentals ▼
Empirical relationships showing that AI performance improves predictably as model size, training data, and compute scale — following power-law curves.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Scaling laws explain why AI companies keep releasing bigger models. More parameters + more data + more compute = better general performance — but with diminishing returns and exponential cost. Model size alone does not determine clinical usefulness.
For Researchers & Educators
Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) showed optimal training balances parameters and tokens roughly 1:20. Emergent capabilities appear at scale thresholds, making scaling both predictable (overall loss) and surprising (capability phase transitions).
Clinical Application
A smaller domain-fine-tuned model often beats a much larger general model on specific clinical tasks. Understand what benchmarks a model was evaluated on before extrapolating performance to your specific use case.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: a drug dose-response curve — predictable relationship between dose (compute) and effect (capability), with therapeutic windows, diminishing returns, and occasional threshold effects at scale.
Related Concepts
Fine-TuningEmergent AbilitiesLatent Space
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Scaling laws arm learners with vocabulary to critically evaluate AI marketing. When a vendor claims ‘our model has 1 trillion parameters,’ scaling laws provide the framework to ask: ‘Trained on what data? For what tasks? Validated against which clinical benchmarks?’
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“Bigger model = better clinical AI.” — Scaling improves general capability, but domain-specific fine-tuning on a smaller model often wins on narrow clinical tasks. Learners should challenge vendor claims about model size without validated, domain-specific clinical benchmarks.

Prompt Mastery

The craft of steering a model toward useful, reliable output.

07 Prompt Engineering Prompt Mastery ▼
The deliberate practice of crafting, structuring, and refining input text to guide an AI toward more accurate, relevant, and useful output.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Prompting is like writing a clear referral letter. A vague ‘please see this patient’ gets a generic response. A structured prompt — context, constraints, desired format — gets a clinically useful answer. Your prompting skill directly determines output quality.
For Researchers & Educators
Prompt engineering exploits the sensitivity of transformers to their input context. Varying instruction framing, few-shot examples, or chain-of-thought can significantly shift the output distribution without changing any model weights.
Clinical Application
In research, prompt engineering defines the quality of AI-assisted tasks: literature screening, data extraction, summarization. Well-specified prompts with explicit criteria, formats, and edge-case rules produce more reproducible, auditable output.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: dosing — the right prompt is the therapeutic window. Too vague is sub-therapeutic. Too restrictive is toxic. Precision in framing unlocks precision in output.
Related Concepts
System PromptFew-Shot PromptingChain-of-ThoughtTemperature
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Prompt engineering is the single highest-leverage teachable skill in AI literacy. Unlike model architecture concepts, prompting can be practiced immediately, feedback is instant, and improvement is visible. Educators who can teach only one AI concept should teach this one.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“The AI knows what I want if I describe it generally.” — LLMs sample from a probability distribution shaped by every word in the prompt. Vague prompts leave enormous probability mass on responses that are plausible but clinically useless. Specificity is not optional — it is the mechanism.

08 System Prompt Prompt Mastery ▼
Hidden instructions provided to a model before the user’s conversation begins, configuring its persona, constraints, knowledge scope, and behavior.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Every clinical AI product has a system prompt you don’t see — it’s what turns a general model into a ‘clinical assistant’ with guardrails. The AI’s refusals, output formats, and behavioral constraints are largely system-prompt-driven, not inherent to the underlying model.
For Researchers & Educators
System prompts occupy a privileged position in the context window, preceding user turns. They configure tool availability, output format, persona, safety constraints, and grounding. They must be documented, versioned, and included in research methods sections.
Clinical Application
The system prompt used for a given research application should be documented, versioned, and included in the methods section — it is part of the experimental protocol. Undocumented prompt changes break reproducibility.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: standing orders and protocols set before a shift — they define what the AI will and won’t do before any patient interaction begins, and they vary entirely by who wrote them.
Related Concepts
Prompt EngineeringGuardrailsRAGPrompt Injection
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
System prompts are invisible to end users but control almost everything about a clinical AI product’s behavior. Teaching this concept de-mystifies why two products using the same underlying model behave so differently, and establishes that documenting system prompts is a reproducibility requirement.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“The AI I’m using is just the underlying model.” — What you see as the ‘AI’ is the underlying model filtered through a system prompt you cannot read. Two products using GPT-4 can behave completely differently based on their system prompts alone.

11 Temperature Prompt Mastery ▼
A parameter that controls the randomness of AI outputs. Low = predictable, deterministic; high = variable, creative.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Temperature is a dial between ‘reliable and conservative’ and ‘creative and unpredictable.’ For clinical decision support, you want low temperature — consistent, safe answers. For brainstorming differentials, slightly higher may generate useful variety.
For Researchers & Educators
Temperature scales the logit distribution before softmax. At T approaching 0 the model greedily selects the highest-probability token. At T greater than 1 the distribution flattens, raising entropy. This is the primary lever for exploring output distribution in systematic research.
Clinical Application
Temperature directly affects reproducibility. Low T yields near-deterministic outputs — essential for systematic reviews or data extraction where consistency matters. Document temperature as part of AI methodology, the same way you document statistical analysis parameters.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: a rheostat between ‘focused specialist’ (T=0) and ‘creative generalist’ (T=1). Most clinical tasks favor the focused end. Most brainstorming tasks favor the middle range.
Related Concepts
Prompt EngineeringHallucination
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Temperature is the most commonly undocumented parameter in clinical AI use. Learners default to whatever the tool’s default setting is — often without knowing it exists. Teaching temperature awareness enables informed choices about consistency vs. creativity and establishes that temperature is a methods variable that must be reported.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“Temperature is a setting I don’t need to worry about.” — Every AI tool has a default temperature that shapes every output you receive. For research use, not documenting temperature is equivalent to not reporting your statistical software settings. It makes the work unreproducible.

20 Hallucination Safety & Evaluation ▼
When an AI generates information that sounds plausible and confident but is factually incorrect, fabricated, or unsupported by its training data.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Like a confident colleague who invents a detailed answer — complete with fake references — instead of admitting uncertainty. In clinical AI this means plausible-sounding but wrong diagnoses, doses, or citations. Always verify against primary sources.
For Researchers & Educators
Hallucinations occur when outputs diverge from ground truth due to the probabilistic nature of token prediction. The model optimises for linguistic plausibility, not factual accuracy — yielding confident confabulation, especially on rare facts or out-of-distribution queries.
Clinical Application
A critical validity threat in research. AI-assisted literature extraction or data synthesis can introduce fabricated citations, distorted effect sizes, or non-existent studies. Any AI output used in a research protocol requires systematic verification.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: a medical student who, rather than saying ‘I don’t know,’ invents a plausible-sounding answer with fake references — because they’re optimised to always give an answer.
Related Concepts
RLHFGuardrailsTemperaturePrompt Engineering
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Hallucination is the most important AI safety concept for clinical educators. Every other safety concept (RAG, guardrails, RLHF, governance) exists partly to address hallucination. It is the one concept that, if not understood, can directly harm patients through wrong clinical information.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“If the AI sounds confident, it’s probably right.” — Confidence and accuracy are decoupled in LLMs. The model generates what is statistically most likely to follow — which is often confident-sounding language regardless of factual accuracy. Confident tone is a linguistic property, not an epistemic signal.

21 RLHF Safety & Evaluation ▼
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — training where humans rate AI outputs and those ratings fine-tune the model toward more helpful, accurate, safe behavior.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians

22 Guardrails Safety & Evaluation ▼
Safety constraints built into an AI system — via training, system prompts, or filters — that prevent harmful, dangerous, or off-policy outputs.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Guardrails are why clinical AI won’t prescribe opioids or provide lethal-dose info on request. They’re safety rails baked in by the developer. But guardrails vary enormously — a model with weak guardrails deployed clinically poses real risk.

Clinical Analogy

⚠️ Misconception“If a clinical AI product has guardrails, it’s safe to use.” — Guardrails range from hard technical stops to easily bypassed soft filters. ‘This product has guardrails’ says nothing about their calibration, adversarial robustness, or failure modes. 24 Bias (AI) Safety & Evaluation ▼
Systematic errors in AI output arising from biases in training data or model design, leading to unfair, inaccurate, or discriminatory performance across groups.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check

Emergence is debated: real non-linearity, or an artefact of discontinuous metrics? Either way, certain capabilities (CoT reasoning, instruction following) show threshold-like transitions with scale.
Clinical Application
A model at one scale may have a fundamentally different capability profile than a newer, larger version. Continuous re-evaluation of AI used in longitudinal research is necessary as models update.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: quantum effects in biology — behaviors that seem impossible at small scales become dominant at larger ones, following different rules.

Emergent abilities explain why AI governance frameworks must include continuous monitoring, not just pre-deployment validation. A model that passed your validation at one scale threshold may exhibit qualitatively different behaviors at the next scale level.

⚠️ Misconception“If the AI couldn’t do something last year, it still can’t.” — Emergent capabilities appear discontinuously with scale. 27 Human-in-the-Loop Workflow & Governance ▼
A design principle where a human explicitly reviews and approves AI outputs at critical decision points, rather than allowing fully autonomous action.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
The clinical standard: AI flags, humans decide. For drug dosing, diagnosis, triage — the physician remains the decision-maker. Improves safety and preserves clear accountability. Fully autonomous clinical AI is rarely appropriate.
For Researchers & Educators
HITL sits on a spectrum from full human control to full autonomy. Optimal configuration depends on task stakes, model error rate, expert throughput, latency. HITL also generates labeled data for ongoing evaluation and improvement.
Clinical Application
Core requirement in regulated research. For eligibility screening, protocol deviations, endpoint adjudication: define explicitly which steps require human review, who is qualified, and how disagreements are resolved.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: a co-pilot — the AI flies but the human pilot monitors and takes over when needed. The question is always: when does the pilot take the controls?

28 Orchestration Workflow & Governance ▼
The coordination layer managing multiple AI models, tools, and agents working together in a complex automated workflow.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
Orchestration makes AI pipelines possible — one system routes a clinical question to the right model, retrieves guidelines, checks interactions, synthesises a response. The orchestration layer determines how components work together and where failures occur.
For Researchers & Educators
Frameworks like LangChain, LlamaIndex, AutoGen manage prompt construction, tool selection, output parsing, state, and error handling across multi-agent systems. Formal verification of orchestration logic is an active research area for safety-critical applications.
Clinical Application
Orchestration determines end-to-end reliability. A failure at any orchestration node — retrieval, synthesis, formatting — can corrupt downstream results without obvious error signals. Comprehensive logging at each step is essential.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: an OR coordinator — doesn’t perform the surgery but ensures the right team, equipment, and sequence are in place for the procedure to succeed.

Why This Concept Matters for Teaching

Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“If each component of my AI pipeline works, the whole pipeline works.” — Integration failures are distinct from component failures. An orchestration error can corrupt the entire pipeline output even when each individual component works correctly.

29 Governance (AI) Workflow & Governance ▼
Policies, processes, oversight, and accountability frameworks that ensure AI systems are used safely, ethically, and in compliance with regulations.
Definition
Why Teach This
How to Teach
Knowledge Check
For Clinicians
AI governance is why hospitals need policies on which AI tools physicians can use, with what data, and with what oversight. Any diagnostic test requires validation before clinical use — AI tools require a framework for selection, validation, monitoring, and accountability.
For Researchers & Educators
Governance covers technical (model cards, eval frameworks, audit logs) and organisational (institutional policies, review boards, incident reporting) layers. Regulatory frameworks (FDA AI/ML-SaMD, EU AI Act) increasingly mandate governance for healthcare AI.
Clinical Application
Research governance includes IRB review where applicable, data-governance agreements, methods documentation for reproducibility, ongoing performance monitoring, and procedures for AI-related adverse events. Pre-register AI methods where possible.
Clinical Analogy
Think of it like: the FDA drug-approval pathway — not every AI tool needs the same oversight, but risk-stratified review ensures high-stakes applications receive appropriate scrutiny before deployment.
Related Concepts
Human-in-the-LoopGuardrailsData PrivacyBias (AI)
Why This Concept Matters for Teaching
Governance is the culminating concept of the curriculum — it integrates all previous concepts into an institutional framework. Learners who have worked through the entire curriculum are now equipped to design and advocate for appropriate governance structures at their institutions.
Common Misconception to Address
⚠️ Misconception“Good governance means prohibiting AI until it’s perfectly safe.” — Perfect safety is not achievable, for AI or any clinical tool. Good governance means risk-stratified oversight calibrated to the stakes of the decision, the quality of the evidence, and the robustness of the safety framework — not prohibition.
